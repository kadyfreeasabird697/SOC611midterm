L1 · What is Sociology?
Sociology = scientific study of society

Sociological imagination (Mills)

  • Relate private troubles to public issues; see the general in the particular.
  • Biography = intersection of society & history.
  • Ex: divorced man → divorce rates, women's employment, marriage expectations; Taylor Swift "The Man" → gendered double standard.
  • 个人困扰 ↔ 公共议题；个人传记 × 历史。

Society & social problems

  • Giddens: people + territory + common political authority + distinct identity.
  • This class ✗ (no political authority, identity ends in Dec). Hong Kong ✓.
  • Social problem: beyond the individual + many concerned. Sociology was born because of social problems.
  • Eufunction (Merton 1949): positive counterpart of dysfunction. Coleman (1966): doctors' networks spread a new drug.

Birth of sociology

  • Comte coined the word (socius + logos). Founders: Marx, Durkheim, Weber.
  • 3 transformations: industrialization, urbanization, democratization.
  • Gemeinschaft → Gesellschaft: tradition, religion, community → individuality, reason, contract.
  • Progress and disorder / alienation.

Three classic approaches

  • Structural‑functional: society as a system → order. Structure = stable pattern; Sewell: schemas + resources.
  • Social‑conflict: inequality → conflict & change (class, gender, race). Rick & Morty's Citadel.
  • Symbolic‑interaction: micro; meanings made in everyday interaction.
  • First two = macro; SI = micro.

L2 · Culture
Culture = what we think + how we act + what we own

Basics

  • Shared, socially transmitted, not biological. (Kroeber & Kluckhohn found 164 definitions.)
  • Nonmaterial (ideas: 濕濕碎, "okay lah") vs material (objects).
  • Culture shock; we take our culture as "natural".
  • Culture (way of life) ≠ nation (political entity) ≠ society (organized interaction). Culture enables society — and resists change.

Levels (Lizardo 2017)

  • Public culture: icons, discourses, institutions.
  • Personal culture: declarative (values, worldviews you can say) / nondeclarative (skills, dispositions, associations — the lemon).
  • Values can be public or personal, depending where you observe them.

Elements

  • Values: standards of desirable. Beliefs: ideas held true because of values.
  • Norms: mores (moral weight) vs folkways (routine, casual).
  • Sanctions: rewards/punishments.
  • World Values Survey: traditional ↔ secular‑rational; survival ↔ self‑expression.
  • Symbol: anything carrying a shared meaning; "doing culture" in interaction.

Cultural capital (Bourdieu 1986)

  • Embodied (accent, taste, formal‑dinner know‑how) · Objectified (books, piano, art) · Institutionalized (degree).
  • Transmission → conversion (school rewards it as "talent") → social reproduction.

Measuring culture (Mohr et al. 2019)

In people

Thought · Talk · Action

Thought: surveys, experiments, IAT/AMP. Vaisey: forced‑choice items tap fast "gut" cognition; interviews tap slow deliberation (later: a continuum). Srivastava & Banaji: implicit self‑concept predicted real collaboration; self‑report didn't.

Talk: interviews → idealized accounts, not behaviour predictions ("Talk Is Cheap", Jerolmack & Khan).

Action: participant observation, digital traces. Polanyi: practice is tacit. Wacquant's boxing gym.

In objects

Production · Diffusion · Consumption

Production of culture: organizations & industry shape what's new. Peterson & Berger: Billboard chart turnover; indie labels as talent scouts.

Diffusion: adopters become adapters (The Nanny abroad).

Consumption: authenticity signalled by object features (grime & cheap beer in a blues club).

In relationships

Interaction · Networks · Fields

Conversation analysis, Goffman. Network tools on people and texts (topic models, word embeddings; Bail's "cultural betweenness").

Bourdieu's fields mapped with (multiple) correspondence analysis.

L3 · Socialization
Socialization = lifelong learning of culture

Nature vs nurture

  • 19th c.: differences explained by nature (biological racism, Darwin quote).
  • 20th c.: Watson — behaviour is learned (Little Albert, unethical).
  • "Nurture is our nature"; like the oranges, behaviour is shaped by environment.
  • Giddings (1897): theory of socialization = "most important part" of sociology.

Agents

  • Family: most important; children tend to conform (a tendency, not copying). Politics, gender roles, religion…
  • School: wider social world, hidden curriculum, first bureaucracy, teachers as role models.
  • Peers: escape adult supervision; anticipatory socialization (groups you want to join).
  • Mass media; new: social media, video games; the state (direct/indirect agent).

Critique & Guhin et al.

  • Parsons: learning "requisite orientations" for a role → seen as cultural downloading.
  • Missing: agency, incoherence, conflict. Lareau: children are active; relation is reciprocal.
  • Guhin: keep the term, drop functionalist baggage (postfunctionalist).

Life course & data

  • Primary (childhood, family) vs secondary (later, outside family). Resocialization: university, job, migration (HKDSE MCQs → essays).
  • Kiley & Vaisey (2020): adult culture mostly settled (change <1%/item).
  • Y = α + βX + γZ + ε: β = socialization effect; Z = selection (homophily); heterogeneous β; timing.

Interaction vs social influence vs socialization — likely MCQ. Interaction = any exchange, one encounter, ends with it. Social influence = change in attitude/behaviour caused by others, can be momentary (Asch), fades when others leave, one specific behaviour. Socialization = learning & internalizing culture, prolonged and repeated, persists without the agent, carries over to new situations. 护士换医院仍保持习惯 → socialization（模拟题6）。
Reading patterns backwards: data → pattern (gap by gender/class) → mechanism (socialization) → test (does the gap track exposure?). Bos et al. 2020: girls show less political ambition already in elementary school. Calarco 2018: middle‑class pupils ask teachers for help, working‑class pupils try not to bother them. But a group gap is not proof of socialization: rivals include discrimination, resources, institutional rules. Avoid deficit thinking.
L4 · Social Interaction in Everyday Life
Social interaction = how people act & react in relation to others

Structure → status

  • Social structure: rules & building blocks (Sewell: like grammar; practice like speech).
  • Status: a position (nominal) or rank (ordinal). Ascribed vs achieved (Instagram followers: "it depends").
  • Status set: all statuses at one time. Master status: shapes your whole identity.

Role

  • Status = what you are; role = what you do.
  • Role set: many roles of one status.
  • Role conflict: roles of two+ statuses clash (student vs part‑time worker).
  • Role strain: roles of one status clash (McDonald's cashier + cook; tutor as mentor + strict grader).

Goffman: dramaturgy

  • Status = part in a play; role = script. Impression management.
  • Front stage, backstage, off‑stage (see diagram). Frank Abagnale in "Catch Me If You Can".

Reality & machines

  • Social construction of reality: Magritte's pipe, "Life Is Beautiful", gender.
  • Thomas theorem: defined as real → real in consequences. Self‑fulfilling prophecy (Merton 1948).
  • ANT (Latour, Callon, Law); Tsvetkova et al. 2024.

Goffman's stage

Front stageActor faces the audience; follows shared norms. Waiter in the dining room; lecturer in class; Frank playing pilot.
BackstageDrop the act, show real feelings, rehearse. Audience not allowed. The kitchen.
Off‑stageOutsiders not part of the performance, may not even know it exists.

Role strain at McDonald's (boat)

Macro: one status, two roles (cashier + cook) → ① worker feels role strain (two sets of expectations at once) → ② switches tasks, makes mistakes, burns out, quits → ③ slow service, errors, turnover → macro: lower profit. Fix at box 1: split into two statuses with specialised roles. 从宏观的岗位设计入手打断链条。

Thomas theorem in action

Bank run

Rumour → everyone withdraws → a healthy bank really fails. Merton's self‑fulfilling prophecy; SVB 2023 (≈$42bn in one day).

Pygmalion effect

Rosenthal & Jacobson 1968: randomly picked "bloomers" got more teacher attention → larger gains.

HK toilet paper, Feb 2020

Rumour of shortage → panic buying → empty shelves despite enough supply.

Are machines part of interaction?

Two answers
No — machines are tools
Interaction needs meaning: both sides interpret each other. A kiosk understands nothing; we act through it. (Weber, symbolic interactionism, Goffman)
Yes — machines take part
If something changes the course of action, it is part of the interaction. (Latour, ANT)

ANT in four words

Sociotechnical networks: the social = links between humans and non‑humans. Symmetry: same vocabulary for both. Actors: anything with effects (person, kiosk, speed bump). Delegation: a speed bump does the police officer's job.

Textbook add‑ons (Macionis ch. 6)

Ethnomethodology (Garfinkel: break rules to reveal them), role exit, idealization, embarrassment & tact, nonverbal communication & personal space, social construction of emotions, language and gender, humor.

Lizardo (2017) · Improving Cultural Analysis

American Sociological Review 82(1): 88–115

Thesis in one sentence. People carry culture in two separate forms — declarative "know‑that" (fast to learn, can be said) and nondeclarative "know‑how" (slowly built through repeated experience, lives in habits and the body) — and because the two are learned through different pathways, they are often dissociated: what people say and what they do can come apart.
人身上的文化有两种形态：能说出来的"知道是什么"和身体里的"知道怎么做"。两者学习途径不同，所以经常脱节——说的和做的不一样，这不是虚伪，而是两套系统。
Picture it · 比喻

Learning to drive. In driving school you memorise rules: "mirror, signal, turn" (declarative, learnt in one lesson). After two years the body just does it (nondeclarative, built from thousands of repetitions). You still know the rule, but it is not what drives the car. A person who only read the manual can explain driving perfectly and still crash. 学车：交规一节课就记住（陈述性），真正会开靠上千次重复（非陈述性）。只读过手册的人能讲得头头是道，但上路照样出错。

The "cultural triangle" — three relations any cultural analysis must consider: (a) declarative ↔ nondeclarative inside the person (Vaisey's dual process); (b) declarative ↔ public culture (Swidler's toolkit, strong program); (c) nondeclarative ↔ public culture (practice theory: Bourdieu's habitus–field). Each can be strongly or weakly coupled. 三组关系，各自可以"强耦合"或"弱耦合"。

Lizardo's own examples (the "paradoxes" he dissolves)

1 · Achievement–aspiration paradox

Black and Hispanic youth in the US report aspirations as high as white youth, yet achieve less; Asian American youth report modest aspirations and outperform. "Oppositional culture" (Ogbu) and "Asian values" theories fail because they look only at declarative attitudes. The real difference lies in nondeclarative school know‑how ("noncognitive skills", self‑control, habits rewarded by teachers), which needs repeated exposure and so tracks family resources.

"如果你只靠陈述性文化解释，就会死在陈述性文化上。"想要好成绩的愿望人人都有（被美国成功话语同质化），差别在"会读书的身体习惯"。

2 · Autonomy vs obedience (Kohn; Weininger & Lareau 2009)

Middle‑class parents say they value autonomy and negotiate verbally, but in practice they enrol kids in structured, adult‑supervised activities that train conformity to institutional authority. Working‑class parents say "obey", but children have more practical autonomy in unstructured play. Not a paradox: two different enculturation channels.

嘴上教"自主"，身体训练的却是"适应权威"——这正是中产孩子在学校吃得开的原因。

3 · Cultural omnivores (Peterson & Kern)

Elites declare openness to all genres ("I like everything"), yet engage with culture in class‑marked ways (preferring complexity, authorial intention). Declarative discourse of tolerance, nondeclarative distinction.

4 · Beyond groupism

Use the same kind of mechanism to explain both advantage and disadvantage; don't use "culture" for Asians' success but "structure" for Black underachievement. Link each piece of personal culture to public culture separately, as an empirical hypothesis.

Use it on a social issue 怎么套用

Environmental behaviour in HK. Most people agree "we should reduce plastic" (declarative, from campaigns, one exposure enough). But grabbing a plastic bag at the wet market or ordering takeaway is a nondeclarative routine. Policy: change the environment so new habits form through repetition (plastic‑bag levy, default "no cutlery"), not more slogans.
Gender equality at home. Young couples say housework should be shared; in practice women still do more. The "know‑how" of who notices the dirty dishes was learnt over a childhood of watching mothers. Surveys capture the saying; time‑use diaries capture the doing.
DSE and university. Grassroots students believe "education changes fate" as strongly as anyone, but may lack the embodied know‑how of asking tutors, networking, office hours (cf. Calarco). Inequality hides in the nondeclarative layer.
Measuring discrimination. Landlords say they never consider ethnicity (declarative) but reply less to South Asian names in an audit study (nondeclarative practice). Mock Q5. Choose methods that match the form of culture.

Guhin, Calarco & Miller‑Idriss (2021) · Whatever Happened to Socialization?

Annual Review of Sociology 47: 109–129

Thesis in one sentence. Socialization fell out of favour because it was tied to Parsons' functionalism (a "cultural downloading" model that ignored power, agency, heterogeneity and fed "culture of poverty" victim‑blaming), but most critiques hit Parsons, not the process; dropping the word costs sociology power, history and transferability, so we should keep it in a postfunctionalist form.
社会化这个词因为和帕森斯功能主义绑在一起而失宠，但批评针对的是功能主义而不是社会化过程本身。扔掉这个词会丢掉"权力、历史、可迁移性"，所以应该以"后功能主义"的方式把它带回来。
Picture it · 比喻

Downloading vs learning a language. The old model treats a child like a phone downloading an app: society installs values, the child runs them. The new model is more like a child learning Cantonese in a Mandarin‑speaking family in Hong Kong: she absorbs, mixes, resists and invents slang with friends (agency, heterogeneity) — but she did not choose which languages surround her, and some accents carry more status (power). Guhin says: keep the word "socialization", but use it like language learning, not app downloading. 旧模型像手机下载APP；新模型像在香港普通话家庭长大的孩子学粤语——会吸收、混用、反抗、和朋友造新词（能动性、异质性），但她没得选周围是什么语言，而且某些口音更"高级"（权力）。

Four lines of critique (against Parsons)

Power

Parsons explained order by shared values; critics: when values align, it is because of power (Giddens, Cohen). Bourdieu: conflict is source and outcome.

Agency

Wrong (1976): the "oversocialized" actor has no inner conflict. Childhood scholars (Corsaro, Pugh): children interpret and resist adult culture.

Heterogeneity

Gender is an ongoing interactional achievement, not a role fixed in childhood (West & Zimmerman, "doing gender"). People carry diverse toolkits (Swidler).

Politics

"Culture of poverty" (Lewis 1959; Moynihan 1965) used socialization to blame families → deficit thinking, hiding racism and resource hoarding.

What gets lost without the word

Power

Socialization captures "being brought into a social world not of your own choosing, over which you lack control" — children in families, students in classrooms, people of colour in White settings. Racial socialization (fathers preparing sons for racism), gendered socialization in preschool (Gansen), hidden curriculum harming Black children (Tyson).

History

Focusing only on agency and toolkits treats culture as "simply available". We lose how people came to know the culture they use — the "why", not only the "how" (Vaisey).

Transferability

Bourdieu's vocabulary is "idiosyncratic and unwieldy" (citing Lizardo); Corsaro's term never left childhood studies. "Socialization" is understood across psychology, political science, education.

New directions (postfunctionalist agenda)

  • Bodies and brains: cognitive science, dual‑process models, Lizardo (2017) — links directly to reading 1. Secondary socialization of adults (Luft: combat socialization produces dehumanization; Winchester: adult converts).
  • Language & conversation analysis: language socialization is embodied and less one‑sided than it looks.
  • Pragmatism (Dewey): habits are means, and break down when obstructed → reflexivity and change.
  • Values (Miles 2015): values sit in automatic cognition and are causally linked to behaviour.
  • Political socialization & ideology: Bos et al. 2020 (boys show more political ambition already in elementary school); immigrants' political socialization.
  • Model: Winchester & Guhin (2019) "normative frames" — evangelical students and converts learn not just a tool (prayer) but when and how to use it and how to judge success, inside an organization's power.

Use it on a social issue

Ethnic minority students in HK schools. South Asian students are socialized into Chinese‑language school norms they did not choose (power). They adapt, mix languages, form peer cultures (agency, heterogeneity). Blaming "their family culture" for low results would repeat the culture‑of‑poverty mistake; look at Chinese‑language policy and school banding.
Mainland students at a HK university. Secondary socialization: new norms of class discussion, Cantonese social life. Not downloading — they choose, resist, form their own groups. Yet institutional power (grading, language) shapes what counts as success.
Gender and STEM. Girls' lower science aspirations: socialization through family, school, social‑media feeds. But gender is "done" repeatedly in interaction, so change is possible; and gaps may also reflect discrimination, not only learning.
Intergenerational poverty (劏房 families). Avoid deficit thinking: don't say poor families "fail to socialize" kids. Show how housing, school resources and labour markets shape what children learn and what they can use.

Tsvetkova, Yasseri, Pescetelli & Werner (2024) · A New Sociology of Humans and Machines

Nature Human Behaviour 8: 1864–1876

Thesis in one sentence. Humans and machines (bots, algorithms, robots) now form one social system of H–H, H–M and M–M interactions; because machines behave differently and change how humans behave, the collective outcomes cannot be deduced from human or machine behaviour alone, so we need a new sociology that studies these hybrid systems as complex systems.
人和机器组成同一个社会系统。机器的行为不同，又会改变人的行为，所以集体结果无法从个体行为简单推出——需要一门研究"人机混合社会"的新社会学。
Picture it · 比喻

A mahjong table with one robot player. Even if nobody knows the robot is there, the game changes because it plays differently (1st order). If players know, they bluff and cooperate differently with it (2nd order). And the humans start playing differently with each other after watching it (3rd order) — like Go players after AlphaGo. 一张麻将桌上坐着一个机器人：即使没人知道，牌局也会变（第一层）；知道后人对它的打法不同（第二层）；看过它打牌后，人和人之间的打法也变了（第三层）——就像AlphaGo之后的棋手。

Diagram · one hybrid network

From machines as media (tools humans communicate through) to machines as actors (many, independent, heterogeneous agents that act, react and interact). The aggregate is a complex system: network effects and nonlinear dynamics. 从"机器是媒介"到"机器是行动者"。

How humans and machines differ

Machines vs humans
Machines: predictable, persistent, precise, fast, better informed (global information), less adaptable and less susceptible to influence.
Humans: local information, satisfice, make errors, learn and adapt, succumb to peer pressure, yet stubborn; cognitive biases (which algorithms trained on human data may copy).

We treat machines like people… (CASA)

Politeness, reciprocity, gender & racial stereotypes, distress when a robot is mistreated; robots cause informational & normative conformity; we often trust algorithmic advice more than human advice.

…but not quite

Flatter emotions (less gratitude, anger, fairness); more rational and selfish — cooperate less, exploit more; judge machine mistakes more harshly; groups even bully robots.

Three orders of difference 三层差异（高频考点）

1st
Machines behave differently from humans. Even if nobody notices the bots, outcomes change. 机器本身行为不同。
2nd
Humans interact differently with machines; machines interact with each other, even when not designed to (Wikipedia bots reverting each other). 人对机器的互动方式不同；机器之间也互动。
3rd
Machines change how humans behave and interact with each other. Just suspecting a bot is present changes behaviour; AlphaGo changed human Go strategies. 机器的存在改变了人与人之间的互动。

Five classic situations, now hybrid

Competition

Trading algorithms raise efficiency and liquidity but can amplify crashes (2010 flash crash); pricing algorithms can learn to collude; sniping bots drive amateurs away.

Coordination

Slightly random ("noisy") bots in central positions help stuck groups reach agreement faster. Human‑imitating bots that don't adapt make things worse.

Cooperation

A few hidden, persistently cooperative bots raise group cooperation (humans wait for others to cooperate first). Known bots: people cooperate less.

Contagion

Twitter bots amplify misinformation, negativity, polarization. Weak bots spread further because they don't alienate followers → cascades. Spiral of silence.

Collective decisions

Machines add diversity: AlphaGo pushed Go players to new strategies; hybrid human+machine diagnosis and citizen science improve accuracy but can lower volunteer engagement.

What a machine does depends on: how many there are · where they sit in the network · whether they are covert or overt (covert bots are more problematic). Four case boxes: high‑frequency trading, Twitter, Wikipedia (registered, diverse bots → resilient), Reddit (AutoMod; humans copy bots' words and sentiment).
Research

Agent‑based models distinguishing human and bot agents; experiments comparing H–M, human‑only and machine‑only groups; field interventions; ethics of covert bots.

Design

Design machines separately for H–M and M–M interaction; rule hierarchies (adapt Asimov's laws); local culture; complex‑adaptive principles (negative feedback, modularity).

Policy

Treat AI as a multiplicity of machines, not one existential threat. Protect diversity of H–M ecologies (similar algorithms → flash crashes); demand resilience; anticipate co‑development (care robots reduce caregivers' empathy).

Link to Coleman's boat (the teacher made this link on the slide): machines enter at the micro level, but their effects show up at the macro level. A few weak social‑media bots → small nudges to individual users → cascades through the network → polarized public opinion. 作文里把这条链画出来，立刻显得有理论深度。

Use it on a social issue

Misinformation & elections. Covert bots retweet partisan content, target central users; weak direct influence, strong collective effect via cascades and spiral of silence → polarization (2016 US election, Brexit, Catalonia).
Care robots for HK's ageing population. Robots can raise social interaction among older adults (contagion), but outsourcing care can reduce caregivers' empathy (co‑development). Policy: keep human carers in the loop.
Generative AI in university study. Students trusting ChatGPT (algorithm appreciation) → similar answers across a class → less diversity (collective decision‑making); AI may also nudge human discussion norms (3rd order).
Food‑delivery and ride‑hailing platforms. Pricing and dispatch algorithms compete and coordinate with riders and restaurants; algorithmic collusion can raise prices; riders change how they treat each other (sharing tips to "beat" the algorithm).

How the three readings connect 三篇如何串起来（综合题必备）

Lizardo explains what is learned (two forms of personal culture). Guhin explains how it is learned and why the concept matters (socialization, power, history). Tsvetkova adds a new agent: machines that act inside social systems. Example sentence: "Recommendation algorithms are a new agent of socialization (Guhin): repeated feed exposure builds nondeclarative tastes and associations (Lizardo), and because bots interact with humans and each other, the aggregate effect on public opinion cannot be read off individual users (Tsvetkova)."

一句话串联：算法是新的社会化代理人（Guhin）；反复刷到的内容塑造非陈述性的品味和联想（Lizardo）；而机器与人、机器与机器的互动让总体结果无法从个体推出（Tsvetkova）。
